Research → Teaching Integration

My research is the syllabus. And the syllabus is open to students.

Every question I study — why an infusion chair sits empty while patients wait, what happens to care when a nursing home changes owners, whether a redesigned scorecard moves real resources — starts as a live research problem and ends as something a student can open, run, and argue with in a classroom.

This page runs the loop in both directions. The first half shows what each research stream became in class. The second half breaks those same streams into nine tracks students can actually join — each one listing what you would do, what you need before you start, an honest check on whether it crosses your interests, and where it leads after graduation.

How the Loop Works

I do not treat research and teaching as two jobs competing for the same week. A finding that cannot be explained to a sophomore is usually a finding I do not yet understand, and a classroom exercise that cannot survive contact with real data is usually a story rather than a lesson. Three commitments hold the loop together.

Commitment 01

Every paper owes the classroom an artifact

When a study finishes, it leaves behind something students can touch — a simulator, a dataset, a handbook chapter, a decision the class has to make with incomplete information. The seventeen simulations and handbooks on this site are all residue from research questions, not decorations added afterward.

Commitment 02

Students see the unfinished version

Published work hides its own history: the measure that failed, the specification that collapsed, the interview that changed the question. Students who join a track see the draft while it is still wrong. That is the part of research a textbook cannot transmit.

Commitment 03

The work has to be worth their time even if they leave

Most students who start will not finish a paper with me, and that is a normal outcome rather than a failure. So each track is built to pay out early — a skill, a portfolio artifact, a clearer sense of what they do and do not want — from the first month, not from an eventual publication.

Four Levels of Involvement

Nobody starts at the top and nobody is expected to reach it. Each level is a complete unit with its own payoff, so stopping after Level 1 is a legitimate ending rather than a dropout. Move up only when the previous level went well for both of us.

Level 1

Explorer

2–3 hrs/week · 3–4 weeks
  • Read one paper from the track and one paper it argues with
  • Run the matching simulator or open the public dataset
  • Write one page: what you would test next, and what would change your mind
You walk away withWritten feedback on your one-pager, and a genuine answer to whether research suits you. No prior experience assumed.
Level 2

Contributor

4–6 hrs/week · one semester
  • Own a defined, checkable task: cleaning, a codebook, a literature table, one constructed measure
  • Keep a reproducible script that runs top to bottom
  • Biweekly 30-minute check-in with a written agenda
You walk away withA named acknowledgment in the project's data documentation, a reference letter grounded in specifics, and a portfolio artifact.
Level 3

Analyst

6–10 hrs/week · two semesters
  • Own an analytical component end to end — a robustness table, a scenario set, a mapping module
  • Defend your choices when I push back on them
  • Draft the methods paragraph describing your own work
You walk away withConference abstract or poster co-authorship where the contribution supports it, and INFORMS/DSI student session experience.
Level 4

Co-author

10+ hrs/week · a year or more
  • Own a question inside the stream: design, execution, writing
  • Typically an honors thesis, capstone, or funded RA appointment
  • Respond to referee comments alongside me
You walk away withManuscript co-authorship when the contribution meets the criteria below, a writing sample, and doctoral application support.

Track 01 SimulationPython / AnyLogicHealthcare

Oncology Outpatient Flow: Making an Infusion Center Stop Waiting

Builds on Enhancing Efficiency and Workflow in Oncology Outpatient Services by Simulation-Based Optimization (forthcoming, Annals of Operations Research) and the follow-on deep reinforcement learning scheduling paper, from a two-year collaboration with the Cancer Institute of New Jersey.
An infusion center has chairs, nurses, pharmacists, and a schedule that looks balanced on paper. Patients still wait for hours, and the wait is worst in the middle of the day for reasons no one on the floor can name from memory. The research answer is that the schedule is optimized against averages while the floor experiences variance. Turning that answer into a defensible recommendation takes a model somebody has to build, validate, and stress — which is exactly the work available here.
What you would actually do
  • Fit arrival and service-time distributions from anonymized operational timestamps, and argue for the ones you chose
  • Build or extend a discrete-event model in SimPy or AnyLogic; validate it against observed throughput before trusting a single output
  • Run scenario grids — chair counts, nurse ratios, appointment templates — and find where the curve bends
  • Turn one scenario set into a classroom-ready demo that students in my analytics course actually use
Prerequisites before you start
  • Required: one introductory statistics course — you should know what a distribution is and why an exponential tail differs from a normal one
  • Required: Python fundamentals (loops, functions, pandas) or a commitment to work through chapters 1–5 of the Python guide in your first three weeks
  • Helpful: exposure to queueing ideas — try the M/M/c simulator and see whether the behavior surprises you
  • Not required: AnyLogic, healthcare background, prior simulation coursework
Does this cross your interests?
Yes, ifYou are the kind of person who watches a line at a pharmacy and starts diagnosing it. You like building a thing that runs. You accept that the first honest answer is often "it depends on the variance," and you find that interesting rather than annoying.
Probably not, ifModel validation sounds like a chore rather than the actual science. Much of this work is establishing that your model behaves like reality before anyone will believe what it says about a scenario that has never happened.
Where it leads afterward
  • Jobs: hospital operations and process improvement, capacity planning, industrial engineering inside health systems, healthcare consulting, airline and logistics network planning
  • Research: the standard entry path into OR/OM doctoral programs; simulation-optimization, stochastic scheduling, service systems
  • Transferable well beyond healthcare: every appointment, dock, runway, and call center is the same mathematics with different vocabulary

Track 02 Field dataIRBExcel / Stata

Emergency Department Flow: Where the Boarding Delay Actually Comes From

Builds on Improving Emergency Department Flow (IRB approved, data collection in progress, with clinical co-investigators) and Systems Approach to Addressing ED Congestion.
Emergency department crowding is usually described as an emergency department problem. It frequently is not. A patient boards in the ED because an inpatient bed upstairs is occupied by someone waiting on a discharge decision that waits on a transport that waits on a signature. Locating the true constraint requires timestamp data that nobody collected for research purposes, in a regulated environment where every step is documented. This track is where students learn what research looks like when it is governed.
What you would actually do
  • Build the evidence table: what the ED crowding literature has already established, and where it disagrees with itself
  • Clean and reconcile timestamp records — arrival, triage, decision, admission, departure — where the same event carries three different clocks
  • Construct the operational measures: boarding time, left-without-being-seen rate, door-to-provider intervals
  • Maintain the protocol documentation and data-handling log that makes the analysis auditable
Prerequisites before you start
  • Required: CITI human-subjects research training, completed before touching any data — free, online, roughly four to six hours, and it stays on your résumé
  • Required: reliability. In a governed project, a missed handoff is a compliance event, not an inconvenience
  • Required: confident spreadsheet work; Stata, R, or Python for the cleaning stage, learnable on the way in
  • Not required: clinical background, statistics beyond an introductory course, prior IRB exposure
Does this cross your interests?
Yes, ifYou are pre-health, pre-MPH, or heading toward health policy and want a quantitative edge that most applicants do not have. You want to see how a real study gets approved, monitored, and documented — not just how it gets analyzed.
Probably not, ifYou want to move fast. Regulated research is slow by design: approvals precede access, and access precedes analysis. If waiting three weeks for a data agreement would frustrate you, take a public-data track instead.
Where it leads afterward
  • Jobs: clinical research coordinator, health system analytics, quality and safety departments, hospital administration fellowships, healthcare consulting
  • Research: health services research, health economics, health policy doctoral programs; the CITI credential and documented IRB experience are read as real signals
  • For medical school applicants: a rare kind of research experience — systems-level rather than bench-level, and directly relevant to the wards you will work on

Track 03 Panel econometricsPublic dataStata / R

Workforce Fissuring: What Contract Staffing Does to Service Quality

Builds on Balancing Workforce Fissuring and Service Quality: Evidence from Healthcare Operations (R&R round 2, Journal of Operations Management) and Do Cross-Level Staffing Patterns Influence Clinician Turnover and Care Operations.
Organizations increasingly meet demand with workers they do not employ — agency nurses, contract drivers, platform labor. The cost accounting for this is straightforward. The operational accounting is not: a facility that fills a gap with agency staff may be buying flexibility and selling continuity, and continuity is where service quality lives. Federal payroll-based staffing records make this measurable at daily resolution for thousands of facilities, and that dataset is public. Anyone reading this can download it today.
What you would actually do
  • Assemble panel datasets from CMS Payroll-Based Journal staffing files — millions of daily records, merged across quarters and facility identifiers that do not always agree
  • Construct the core measures: agency staffing share, turnover, hours per resident day, staffing volatility
  • Replicate descriptive figures, then run fixed-effects specifications and learn why the clustering choice changes the answer
  • Write the codebook — the document that lets a stranger rebuild your dataset without asking you a single question
Prerequisites before you start
  • Required: one regression course. You should be able to interpret a coefficient, a standard error, and a dummy variable interaction without looking them up
  • Required: Stata or R at a working level — or a serious start on the Stata guide or R guide on this site
  • Strongly recommended: read the fixed effects and difference-in-differences chapters of the Causal Inference Handbook before our first meeting
  • Not required: healthcare knowledge, labor economics coursework, prior panel data experience
Does this cross your interests?
Yes, ifYou care about how work is organized — gig economy, outsourcing, contingent labor — and you want to argue about it with evidence rather than anecdote. You accept that in this kind of paper the identification strategy is the contribution, and the coefficient is just the last line.
Probably not, ifYou want the modeling to be the interesting part. Realistically, seventy percent of this work is merging, checking, and defending a dataset. The regression is one afternoon. People who love the seventy percent become excellent empirical researchers.
Where it leads afterward
  • Jobs: economic consulting (Analysis Group, Cornerstone, Charles River), people and workforce analytics, policy research organizations such as Mathematica, RAND, or Urban, and healthcare strategy groups
  • Research: the most direct preparation on this page for OM, applied economics, or health policy doctoral study — panel data fluency is the price of admission
  • Portfolio: a public replication package on GitHub, which is worth more in an interview than a transcript line

Track 04 Event studyPublic dataExcel to start

Ownership Transitions: What Changes When a Facility Is Sold

Builds on Engagement Without Responsibility: Ownership Transitions and Stakeholder Welfare in U.S. Nursing Homes and Private Equity in Healthcare: Ownership Transitions and Operational Performance in Global South Health Systems.
A nursing home changes hands. The building is identical, the residents are the same people, the staff mostly stay through the transition. Within eighteen months, staffing hours, deficiency citations, and reported quality can all move — and the direction is contested in a literature with real policy stakes. Testing this credibly demands something unglamorous first: knowing exactly who owned what, and when. Ownership chains run through holding companies and shared management firms, and the public records disagree with each other. Building that ledger honestly is where the research actually happens.
What you would actually do
  • Assemble ownership-change events from CMS provider and change-of-ownership files, then hand-verify a sample against secondary sources
  • Trace ownership chains — operator, property owner, management company — and document every judgment call you make
  • Build event windows and produce the pre/post descriptive plots that show whether anything moved at all
  • Draft a short policy memo translating one finding for a reader with no econometrics training
Prerequisites before you start
  • Required: genuine attention to detail. This track rewards the student who notices that two records list the same facility under slightly different names
  • Required: confident spreadsheet work. You can begin in Excel alone and add Stata or R once the ledger is built
  • Helpful: knowing roughly what a difference-in-differences design is — the handbook covers it, and you can learn it while working
  • Not required: finance coursework, econometrics beyond an introductory course, coding on day one
Does this cross your interests?
Yes, ifYou are interested in the intersection of finance and healthcare, or in business ethics with numbers attached. If the question "who is accountable when ownership is a chain of holding companies?" strikes you as important, this is your track — and it is the most accessible entry point on this page for a student with no coding background.
Probably not, ifRecord linkage sounds tedious to you. It genuinely is tedious. It is also the reason the resulting paper can be believed, and the students who do it well develop judgment that no methods course teaches.
Where it leads afterward
  • Jobs: healthcare investment and commercial due diligence, regulatory and compliance analytics, policy analysis, ESG and impact research, litigation support
  • Research: event-study and difference-in-differences design fluency transfers to finance, strategy, health policy, and law-and-economics questions
  • Underrated skill: data provenance — being the person who can say exactly where every row came from is a career asset in any analytics organization

Track 05 Measurement designPublic dataPython / R

When the Scorecard Changes: How a Rating Redesign Moves Real Resources

Builds on When the Scorecard Changes: Quality Measurement Redesign and Operational Resource Reallocation in Nursing Homes, When Stars Fall: Quality Instability, Consumer Choice, and the Demand for Long-Term Care, and the chain-level yardstick competition study in the dialysis industry.
A regulator changes how a star rating is computed. No facility changed, no patient changed, but thousands of ratings move overnight — and managers respond by moving staff toward whatever the new formula counts. This is Goodhart's law with a federal dataset attached, and it is one of the cleanest natural settings for studying how measurement systems govern behavior. It is also the track where students most often discover that the metrics they have trusted all their lives were design choices someone made in a room.
What you would actually do
  • Reconstruct how measure definitions and weights changed across scorecard versions, and build the timeline nobody has assembled in one place
  • Construct rating volatility measures — how much of a facility's score movement is performance and how much is formula
  • Build a reproducible pull of public rating archives instead of a folder of manually downloaded spreadsheets
  • Build the sensitivity tool: re-weight the components, watch the rankings reshuffle, and see how fragile a public ranking really is
Prerequisites before you start
  • Required: Python or R for data wrangling — this track involves many files with inconsistent structure across years
  • Required: comfort with index construction and weighted averages; the arithmetic is simple, the bookkeeping is not
  • Helpful: any prior encounter with a KPI system you thought was badly designed. That instinct is the research question
  • Not required: causal inference coursework, healthcare knowledge, prior scraping experience
Does this cross your interests?
Yes, ifYou are drawn to metrics, incentives, and gaming — the mechanics of how organizations respond to being scored. Students heading into product analytics, performance management, or strategy find this track speaks their language almost immediately.
Probably not, ifYou want to work directly with people or clinical settings. This is a desk-and-dataset track. The subject is human behavior, but you observe it through administrative records rather than in a hospital.
Where it leads afterward
  • Jobs: performance management, KPI and scorecard design, business intelligence, product analytics, accreditation and ratings organizations, regulatory analytics
  • Research: incentive design, measurement validity, and quality competition — questions that cross OM, accounting, strategy, and public policy
  • The durable lesson: you will never again accept a dashboard metric without asking who chose the weights and what happens when they change

Track 06 Survey methodsWriting-heavyBehavioral

Clinician Empowerment: The Operational Resource Nobody Budgets For

Builds on Exploring the Triple Aim Through Clinician Empowerment: The Role of Resources and Health Disparities on Quality Outcomes (R&R, Journal of Operations Management) and From Triple Aim to Quadruple Aim.
Operations models usually treat the server as a machine with a service rate. Clinicians are not. They exercise discretion, absorb the system's failures personally, burn out, and leave — and the leaving shows up months later as a capacity problem nobody traced back to its cause. The Quadruple Aim adds clinician wellbeing to the classic triple of cost, quality, and experience. Studying it means measuring things you cannot observe directly, which is a genuine methodological craft: constructs, scales, reliability, validity.
What you would actually do
  • Synthesize the literature on empowerment, autonomy, and burnout into a construct table — definition, source, and how each was measured
  • Assemble and pilot survey scales, then test reliability and internal structure
  • Code open-ended responses into categories, and establish that a second coder reaches the same categories
  • Draft the measurement section of a manuscript, which is a writing sample you can show anyone
Prerequisites before you start
  • Required: strong writing. This is the most writing-intensive track on the page, and the writing is the contribution
  • Required: introductory statistics; reliability analysis in R, Stata, or SPSS is learnable once you are here
  • Required: patience with careful reading — you will read thirty papers before you write one table
  • Not required: advanced statistics, coding fluency, healthcare background
Does this cross your interests?
Yes, ifYou come from organizational behavior, human resources, psychology, or nursing, or you are simply more interested in the people delivering a service than in the throughput numbers. This is the natural entry point for students whose strength is reading and writing rather than code.
Probably not, ifYou need to observe your variables directly. Working with latent constructs means accepting that "empowerment" is something you approximate through indicators and defend, never something you measure the way you measure a wait time.
Where it leads afterward
  • Jobs: people analytics, organizational development, healthcare administration, employee experience roles, survey and market research
  • Research: the standard preparation for organizational behavior, human resources, industrial-organizational psychology, and behavioral operations doctoral programs
  • Transferable: anyone who ever builds an employee engagement survey and wants it to mean something needs exactly this skill set

Track 07 GeospatialPublic dataPython / R / Tableau

Access and Equity: Being Near a Service Is Not the Same as Reaching It

Builds on Enhancing Healthcare Operation in Disadvantaged Communities (under review, Health Care Management Science) and the health-disparities component of the clinician empowerment study.
Two neighborhoods sit four miles from the same hospital. One reaches it in twelve minutes by car; the other takes an hour on two buses with a transfer that does not run on Sundays. Straight-line distance says they are equivalent. Every operational outcome says they are not. This track treats access as an operations variable that can be measured properly — and it produces the most visually persuasive artifacts of any track here, because a map argues faster than a regression table.
What you would actually do
  • Merge provider locations with census tract data and deprivation indices, and handle the geographic identifier changes that break naive merges
  • Build access measures that go beyond distance: travel time, provider density per population, catchment overlap
  • Produce the maps — choropleths and service areas that make a disparity visible in one glance
  • Decompose a measured performance gap into the part explained by access and the part that is not
Prerequisites before you start
  • Required: Python (geopandas), R (sf), or serious Tableau skill — plus willingness to learn coordinate systems, which trip up everyone once
  • Required: basic familiarity with census geography, or a week spent acquiring it
  • Helpful: any prior mapping work at all, including a class project
  • Not required: a GIS course, spatial econometrics, healthcare background
Does this cross your interests?
Yes, ifYou want your analysis to be about who gets served rather than only how efficiently. You think visually. You are interested in public health, urban systems, or social policy and want quantitative tools that match the ambition of those questions.
Probably not, ifYou need clean definitions before starting. "Equity" is defined differently by every literature that uses the word, and part of the work here is choosing a definition and defending it in writing rather than assuming one.
Where it leads afterward
  • Jobs: public health analytics, city and state government data teams, health equity roles inside payers and health systems, urban planning, nonprofit and foundation research
  • Research: spatial methods open doors across public health, economics, urban planning, and operations — and remain relatively rare among business school applicants
  • Portfolio: a published map is the single most shareable artifact any of these tracks produces

Track 08 DevelopmentPublic microdataStata

Operations Without a Firm: Extension Services and Smallholder Productivity

Builds on Extension Services, Market Remoteness, and Agricultural Productivity: Evidence from Ethiopian Smallholder Farms (targeting the M&SOM special issue).
Operations management was built around firms with inventory systems, forecasts, and ERP software. Most of the world's production does not look like that. A smallholder farm two hours from the nearest market makes planting, storage, and selling decisions under uncertainty with almost no formal information infrastructure — and an agricultural extension visit is, in operations terms, an information intervention. World Bank household surveys make this measurable, and the microdata is public. This is the track for students who want their operations training pointed at development.
What you would actually do
  • Work with World Bank LSMS-ISA household microdata — multi-wave, multi-module, with household identifiers that must be tracked across rounds
  • Construct market access and remoteness measures from geographic and infrastructure variables
  • Estimate production functions and efficiency scores using stochastic frontier analysis or DEA, and interpret what a frontier actually claims
  • Handle survey weights correctly — the difference between a descriptive statistic that is right and one that is quietly wrong
Prerequisites before you start
  • Required: econometrics through multiple regression, and Stata at a working level
  • Required: tolerance for documentation. Survey microdata comes with hundreds of pages of manuals, and the answers are in there
  • Helpful: any development economics or global health coursework, or lived familiarity with an agricultural economy
  • Not required: frontier analysis experience — SFA and DEA are learnable inside this project
Does this cross your interests?
Yes, ifYou want to work on development, global health, or food systems, and you would rather bring quantitative operations tools to those problems than study them from a distance. International students who know an agricultural region firsthand often bring context that improves the research directly.
Probably not, ifYou want fast iteration. Survey microdata is slow to learn and unforgiving of shortcuts. The first month is mostly reading documentation, and that month is not optional.
Where it leads afterward
  • Jobs: World Bank, IFC, and development finance analytics, international NGO monitoring and evaluation, development consulting, agricultural supply chain and ag-tech roles
  • Research: development economics, agricultural economics, and the growing socially-responsible operations field — where M&SOM and POM now run dedicated special issues
  • Rare combination: operations methods plus development microdata fluency is uncommon enough to be a genuine differentiator

Closing the Loop: Turn a Finding Into the Next Teaching Artifact

Builds on the full set of simulations and handbooks on this site — and on the premise that the last step of a research project is making it teachable.
Every simulator linked from my teaching page started as a research finding that was too abstract to survive a lecture. Someone had to decide which parameters a student should control, which detail to discard, and what has to happen on screen for the insight to land. That translation is intellectually harder than it looks, and it is the fastest way to discover whether you actually understand a result. Students on this track build the artifact the next cohort learns from — with their name on it, in public.
What you would actually do
  • Take one finding from tracks 1 through 8 and identify the single mechanism a student must feel to understand it
  • Design the interaction: which parameters are exposed, what the default case shows, where the surprise happens
  • Build it as a self-contained web simulator or notebook, in the visual language of the existing set
  • Test it on real students, watch where they get confused, and rebuild the part that failed
Prerequisites before you start
  • Required: ability to explain something clearly. This is the actual prerequisite and it is not negotiable
  • Required: HTML and JavaScript, or Python with a notebook interface — intermediate is plenty; existing simulators are readable templates
  • Helpful: having tutored, TA'd, or taught anything at all, including a club workshop
  • Not required: research experience, statistics beyond the finding you are translating, design training
Does this cross your interests?
Yes, ifYou learn by building, or you have ever finished a lecture thinking "there is a better way to show this." Also the right track if you are considering teaching, instructional design, or any career where explaining complex things is the job.
Probably not, ifYou want to be close to the research frontier. This track is one step behind it by design — the finding already exists, and your contribution is making it land. That is a real contribution, but it is not discovery.
Where it leads afterward
  • Jobs: educational technology, corporate learning and development, technical writing, data journalism, developer relations, product roles where explaining is the product
  • Research: pedagogy and simulation-based learning are publishable in their own right; teaching-focused academic careers start with exactly this portfolio
  • Immediate payoff: a public, linkable artifact used by an actual class — the clearest evidence of competence a student can hand an employer

Onboarding: Five Weeks, Self-Paced, No Permission Required

This is the course portion, and it runs whether or not you ever email me. Everything it asks for is already open on this site. Students who arrive having finished week three are working on real tasks within days; students who arrive cold spend their first month here anyway. If you complete week five, send me the output — that single artifact tells me more than a transcript does.

Week 1

Read one paper properly

Pick a track, read its lead paper, then read one paper it argues with. Do not skim the methods — that is where the argument lives.

DeliverableHalf a page: the question, the identification strategy, and the one assumption that would break it.
Week 2

Get the tool working

Install and use whatever your track requires — Stata, R, Python, or a simulator. Use the guides on this site; they were written for exactly this week.

DeliverableA script that runs top to bottom on a fresh machine and produces one figure.
Week 3

Touch the real data

Download the public dataset your track uses. Load it, count rows, find the missing values, and locate the first thing that does not make sense.

DeliverableA one-page data note: source, coverage, three oddities you found, and how you verified them.
Week 4

Reproduce something

Rebuild a published descriptive statistic or figure from that data. It will not match on your first attempt. Finding out why is the entire exercise.

DeliverableYour figure beside the published one, and a paragraph explaining any gap.
Week 5

Propose one extension

Name a question the paper left open that your data could actually address. Be specific about the comparison you would make and what would falsify you.

DeliverableA one-page proposal. Send it with your email — this is the strongest possible application.

Credit, Authorship, Data, and What I Owe You

Research relationships go wrong when expectations stay implicit. These are mine, written down before you start rather than negotiated after the work is done.

Authorship is earned, and discussed in advance

Co-authorship requires a substantial contribution to design, data, or analysis, plus involvement in the writing and the ability to defend the work publicly. We discuss author order before the work starts, not after. Contributions that do not reach that bar are acknowledged by name in the paper — which is a real and citable credit, not a consolation prize. I will never quietly absorb a student's work.

Data rules are absolute, not aspirational

  • Restricted data never leaves approved storage. No personal laptops, no cloud drives, no exceptions
  • Human-subjects projects require completed CITI training before any access
  • Public data is the default entry point precisely so you can start while clearances proceed
  • If you are unsure whether something is permitted, ask first. Asking is never the mistake

Reproducibility is the deliverable

Every task ends in a script that runs from raw data to output without manual intervention. No undocumented spreadsheet edits. If you cannot rerun it next month, it is not finished. This is the habit that separates people who can be trusted with an analysis from people who cannot, and I would rather you learn it here than in your first job.

AI tools are allowed, and disclosed

Use them — I write about teaching with them. Two conditions: you disclose where they were used, and you can defend every line as if you wrote it, because in the ways that matter you did. Code you cannot explain is code we cannot ship. An AI-drafted paragraph you have not verified is a retraction waiting to happen.

Ways to get credit for this

  • Independent study — arranged with your department, graded on the deliverables above
  • Honors thesis or capstone — the natural home for Level 4
  • Paid research assistantship — when grant funding allows; I will tell you plainly whether it does
  • Portfolio only — entirely legitimate, and what most Level 1 and 2 students choose

What I commit to

  • A response to any email that names a specific track
  • A scheduled check-in you can count on, with written feedback on what you submit
  • An honest reference letter with specifics in it — or an early, direct conversation if I cannot write a strong one
  • Telling you when a direction is not working, quickly, rather than letting you spend a semester on it

How to Apply

One email, under two hundred words. No CV required, no formal application, no deadline.

  1. Name the track by number. "Track 4" tells me more than three paragraphs about your interest in healthcare.
  2. State your prerequisites honestly — which you meet, which you do not. A missing prerequisite is a plan, not a disqualification. Overstating one always surfaces in week two.
  3. Two sentences on why the question interests you. Your own words. A generic paragraph reads as generic.
  4. What you want to be able to do in six months. This determines which level and which task I would put you on.
  5. Hours per week and which term you are describing.
  6. Optional but decisive: attach anything you have built — a course project, a script, a week-five proposal from the onboarding sequence above.

Realistic expectations

Openings depend on the term and on where each project sits — a paper in revision needs different help than one in data collection. If nothing fits when you write, I will say so directly and tell you when to check back, rather than leaving you waiting on a maybe.

Public-data tracks (3, 4, 5, 7, 8) rarely have a queue, because the work scales with however many careful people are doing it.

Not a Rutgers student? Public-data tracks work remotely. Clinical and IRB tracks require affiliation and institutional training.

Questions Students Actually Ask
I have never coded. Is there anything here for me?

Yes — Track 4 begins in a spreadsheet, and Track 6 begins with reading and writing. Both build toward code at your own pace rather than demanding it on arrival. The onboarding sequence above also exists precisely for this: five weeks of self-paced work that takes you from nothing to a running script, using free materials already on this site. Students who arrive having done it are not beginners anymore.

Do I need to be a supply chain major?

No, and the mix is usually better when students are not. These tracks have been built to be legible to students from economics, statistics, public health, computer science, nursing, psychology, and business generally. What matters is whether the question holds your attention when the work gets tedious — because it will get tedious, in every track, at some point.

Will I get paid?

Sometimes, when grant funding covers it, and I will tell you plainly whether it does at the moment you ask rather than leaving it vague. Most Level 1 and 2 positions are for credit or for the portfolio. I would rather you know that before you invest a semester than discover it afterward.

What if I start and discover I hate it?

Then Level 1 did its job. Three or four weeks is deliberately short so that leaving costs you almost nothing and teaches you something real about your own preferences. Tell me directly, and I will still write about what you did. The outcome I want to avoid is a student staying six months out of obligation.

Will this help my PhD or medical school application?

It helps in the ways admissions committees can actually verify: a letter that describes specific work rather than general enthusiasm, a writing sample, a reproducible analysis they can look at, and — at Level 3 and above — a conference presentation. It does not substitute for grades or test scores, and I will not claim otherwise. What it reliably provides is evidence that you have done research rather than only taken courses.

How is this different from a class project?

There is no answer key, and no guarantee the question has an answer. Real data arrives broken. A measure you spend three weeks building sometimes has to be discarded. Findings come out null, and a null result still has to be written up honestly. Students often describe this as the first time coursework stopped feeling like a simulation of the thing.

Can I propose my own question instead?

Yes, if it sits close enough to these streams that I can supervise it competently. The realistic path is entering through a track, learning the setting and the data, and then proposing your extension in week five of onboarding or at the end of Level 2. Questions proposed from inside the data are almost always better than questions proposed from outside it.

How much time does this really take?

The hours listed on each level are honest, but they are unevenly distributed. Some weeks are three quiet hours; the week before a submission deadline is not. I plan around your exam schedule when you tell me about it in advance. The most common failure I see is a student taking on Level 3 hours during a semester that could only support Level 1.
